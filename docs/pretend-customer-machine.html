The Pretend Customer Machine

How we count all the pretend customers we could make — and why we don't have to make them all.

Step 1

Six boxes of cards

We want to test a robot that helps people pay their credit card bill.

To test it, we make up pretend customers. Each pretend customer is made by picking one card from each of six boxes.

Step 2

How many pretend customers could we make?

If you have 2 hats and 3 shirts, you can make 2 × 3 = 6 outfits. Same trick here, with six boxes:

4×4×3×9×3×6=7,776

Seven thousand, seven hundred and seventy-six different pretend customers. That's a lot.

Step 3

Step 4

A trick: we don't need every customer. We need every pair.

Imagine a class of kids. You want every kid to sit next to every other kid at least once. You do not need to try every possible seating plan for the whole class — that would take forever. You just need each pair of kids to be next to each other once.

Same with our boxes. Most robot mistakes happen because of two things together — like "in a hurry" and "pays without asking". Not all six at once. So we make sure every two cards from different boxes get to be in the same pretend customer at least once.

How many pairs are inside one pretend customer?

Take the customer from Step 1. It has six cards. A "pair" is any two of those cards together. Let's count them:

5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 15

So every time we run one pretend customer, we tick off 15 pairs from the big list in one go. That's why we need far fewer customers than there are pairs.

How many pairs are there across all six boxes? Count every card-with-card from different boxes:

406pairs in total
Why 406 and not fewer? The "robot mistake" box really has 9 cards, not 6 — three more mistakes belong to other jobs the robot does (like setting up automatic payments) that the machine can't play yet. They still count as pairs we owe, just not ones we can build today.
Step 5

Cross out silly pairs, and set aside ones we can't build yet

Just like Step 3, some pairs are silly — "swap cards" with "one card" is silly as a pair, too. A person went through the list and crossed out 32 of them, writing down why each time.

And 48 pairs use those three "other job" mistakes. They're not silly — we just can't build them until the machine learns those other jobs. We put them in a "not yet" pile so nobody forgets.

Step 6

Pick the fewest customers that show every pair

Now the machine plays a puzzle. It picks one pretend customer that shows lots of pairs. Then another that shows lots of pairs we haven't seen yet. Then another. It keeps going until every one of the 326 pairs has been shown at least once.

Because each customer shows 15 pairs at once, it doesn't take many:

326pairs, all shown by just56pretend customers

Fifty-six. Not four thousand. That's the whole trick.
