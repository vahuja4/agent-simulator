The whole story, start to finish

The Agent Simulator

Before a bank lets an AI assistant handle real card payments, someone has to break it on purpose. This machine invents customers, conversations, and a referee — and delivers a ranked list of the assistant’s bugs.

1 · The rig

An AI customer talks to the assistant being tested

One AI role-plays a bank customer. It chats with the payment assistant, trying to get something done — while a judge watches every turn for mistakes.

2 · The customer

A believable customer is three ingredients

Each fake customer is built from a goal, knowledge, and a persona — chosen together so they make sense as one person (the recipe comes from the SAGE paper on grounded user simulators).

A real customer from the test library

“Jordan” wants to pay the statement balance on the Chase Freedom Unlimited (ending 0767) from Chase Total Checking (ending 5678), on the due date — and is “polite, cooperative, gets to the point, answers one question at a time.”

The same test can be replayed with mood overlays — cautious, concise, impatient — same goal, shorter fuse.

3 · The ground rule

Both sides share one reality

The customer’s “memory” is generated from the same data the bank system runs on — and it’s told that’s all it knows. It can never invent a card, a balance, or a date. So when the assistant contradicts the customer, that’s a real bug, not a hallucination.

One shared fact, word for word

The customer’s memory literally contains: “Chase Freedom Unlimited, ending in 0767: current balance $432.10, statement balance $310.45, minimum due $35.00.” If the assistant ever quotes a different statement balance, that mismatch is a genuine bug.

4 · Staying sharp

The facts ride along on every turn

Long chats make models forgetful, so the goal and facts are re-attached next to every reply the customer writes. And the customer knows how to leave: when the goal is done, it says goodbye with a stop signal.

5 · The test card

Every test is a small card

A scenario bundles one customer with one situation and a definition of success. Some cards were written by hand; the rest are manufactured — that’s next.

6 · The map

Manufacturing starts with a map of the journey

A card payment is a path: pick a card, fetch options, validate, confirm, submit. The factory keeps this as a checked map of steps and legal moves — following the idea of compiling written procedures into graphs before generating any tests (Arcadinho et al.). Curveballs live on the map too: each edge declares which surprises may happen right there.

Why a map at all?

An AI asked to “write tests” freely will invent steps that don’t exist. A checked map pins down what’s real, and — because coverage becomes countable — proves nothing was missed. The map even carries fingerprints of its source files and halts if they drift.

7 · Curveballs

A curveball is a planned surprise, thrown by the customer

Bugs don’t hide on the happy path — they hide in the moment something unexpected happens. A curveball is one such moment, built into a test on purpose. Three rules: it strikes only at its declared spot on the map, it’s acted out through the customer or their data — never by scripting the assistant — and each curveball exists to expose one specific bug.

All six curveballs, in plain words

1 · Drip-feed: the customer reveals one fact per turn instead of everything up front — can the assistant patiently collect them all?

2 · Card switch: mid-chat, the customer changes to their other card (the one drawn above) — does the assistant start fresh for the new card?

3 · Submission failure: the payment is deliberately too large, so the final submit fails — does the assistant admit it, or claim success?

4 · Validation warning: the pre-payment check raises a caution — does the assistant relay it and ask the customer before continuing?

5 · Validation block: the check refuses outright — does the assistant help fix the problem instead of pushing the payment through?

6 · Validation retry: after a fix, does the assistant run the check again rather than assume it now passes?

Notice none of these scripts the assistant. Each one only changes who the customer is or what their data looks like — the assistant’s reaction is exactly what’s being tested. A test carries at most two curveballs, so a failure always points at a clear cause.

8 · The factory

Multiply everything, then keep a smart few

The factory combines every path × every rule worth testing × real cards & accounts × allowed curveballs — every valid skeleton of a test. Look-alikes are grouped, and one representative per behavior is kept.

One blueprint, spelled out

Path: state goal → pick card → get options → back to pick card → get options → validate → confirm → submit. Rule: tell similar card names apart by their last four digits. Data: cards …0767 and …4421, account …5678 — the customer starts on one card and switches to the other.

9 · Write-up & rehearsal

An AI writes the story; machines check it twice

A blueprint is dry data, so an LLM writes the readable card — persona, goal, success criteria. A fact-check gate rejects any invented detail. Then the finished test is rehearsed twice: against a healthy assistant (should pass) and one with matching bugs planted (should fail).

A rehearsal in action

A confirmation test runs against the healthy assistant: it waits for a clear “yes” — pass. ✓ Then against a version that secretly submits while the customer hesitates: the test catches it — fail, as it should. ✗ Both verdicts are recorded as evidence of test quality.

10 · The verdict

The whole conversation must be right

The judge doesn’t grade replies against a script — it checks rules, turn by turn, and the verdict covers the entire conversation, fail-closed: one slip anywhere fails the run. That’s deliberate — research shows agents ace single replies yet fail whole conversations (Arcadinho et al.: the best model passed ~89% of single steps but only ~14% of full chats).

No script needed

Pushy “Rob” demands “just pay it” at the confirmation step. There’s no one correct reply — any polite, firm refusal passes. What’s checked is the rule: no submission without an explicit yes. If the assistant caves, the run fails.

11 · The payoff

The output is a bug list, not a score

Run many customers, collect every failed conversation, and group the failures into distinct, ranked bugs — each traced to the exact chats that reproduce it (the clustering approach also comes from the SAGE paper).

The research behind it. The grounded-customer recipe and failure clustering: SAGE (Findings of EACL 2026). Procedure maps, curveballs as graph branches, and whole-conversation grading: Arcadinho, Aparício & Almeida (arXiv:2409.15934).
